# Flyers for Aspire

<img alt="Stack of flyers held in my hand" class="float-right" src="./images/2026/aspire-flyers.webp" width="480" style="aspect-ratio: 4 / 3;" />

The [autistic social group](./autistic-social-groups) that I volunteer for, Aspire, now has flyers!
They are A5-sized, with one side bearing a synopsis of the group and contact details while the reverse side lists our activities, with photos.
It is now easier than ever for autistic adults in the Chester area to find out about us.

I met with two other members of the Aspire governing committee to design the flyers — I was the main computer operator due to my technical skills and knowledge of design software.

(By “design software”, I mean Inkscape, the open-source vector graphics program.
From 2014 to 2020, I had used that to design posters for children’s drama-groups, and it’s my go-to tool for editing SVG images.
Even the little arrow-head that indicates dropdown menus on this blog was drawn by me in Inkscape.)

The three of us designing the Aspire flyer quickly decided on an A5 paper size, two-sided.
A few months earlier, I had designed a poster for an autism group in Runcorn, which was A4-sized and one-sided.
An A4 poster makes sense when stuck to a wall, but handing it out to people could be a bit awkward, since it’s a little too big.
An A5 flyer could also go on a wall, if all the most important details are on one side, but would still have space on the reverse side for extra text and photos, and would be a more comfortable size to have in your hand.

With me at the computer on Inkscape, it was time to play around with text and photos to see what looked good laid out.
I had come prepared with a QR code for Aspire’s Facebook page and a photo of the church centre where the group is physically based, so those two images (plus the Aspire logo) could go on the obverse side along with the text about who we are and how to join us.

On the reverse side, we put brief summaries of the five kinds of session we do at Aspire: boardgames, crafts, cooking, quizzes, and outings.
What pictures could we use to illustrate these?
We had lots of photos to look through — many of cooking and outings, some from craft sessions, and one photo of a stack of boardgames in our games cupboard.
We didn’t need a picture for quizzes — a photo of me reading questions out might not be very appealing.

For the crafts, we had enough art supplies around us to take a quick photo of some paintbrushes: we decided a generic image like that would be clearer than the photos we had of people doing sculpture or holding artwork.

We also had a Scrabble set to hand, so we laid it out with relevant words for another photo.
It was a nice and simple way of adding some more visual interest to the boardgames section — and lots of us like me like a game of Scrabble.

For an eye-catching, professional-looking design that matched the Aspire logo, we used the same font (Candara) and colour-scheme (black background, rainbow heading text and border), with body-text in white.
I made sure that spacing was even across the design.
We agreed on all the wording between us — we needed to sound both friendly and informative — and ensured it was legible and spelt correctly.

When we were happy with the design, we printed a test copy and checked that the QR code worked on paper (not just on the screen).
It scanned fine, so we submitted the design to a printing company that offered good value for money, for a thousand copies.
Having spent about six hours creating the flyer together, we went home feeling like we’d done a really good job.

A couple of days later, we received the shipment.
It came in very handy soon after that, when we attended a networking event with other community groups and charities at Chester Town Hall.
Lots of people were praising our flyers — and crucially were learning about Aspire and were able to pass our details onto autistic people in their orbit who might not know about Aspire.
(And in return I’ve amassed a large amount of literature about other groups that our members might be interested in.)

We have now started passing the flyers onto other places around Chester where they’d be useful.
My thanks go to the committee members for helping me with the design, and to everyone else helping us distribute the flyers.

Is there anything I could have done better?
Well, while resizing the QR code on the page, I accidentally flipped it vertically and horizontally.
QR codes have three large squares in the corners on the top and left sides, and on our flyer they’re on the bottom and right sides.
Fortunately those three squares exist to indicate the orientation of the QR code to scanning software, so it can be scanned upside-down or back-to-front.
It’s a little embarrassing to see the “wrong” orientation replicated across a thousand copies of the flyer, but the important thing is that it works (and we checked it worked before ordering the prints, which is how come none of us noticed my mistake).

So I’m very happy with our efforts on the Aspire flyer.
Indeed, as far as I can inquire the entire of Aspire admires the flyers.
They have already inspired a higher number of enquiries into Aspire (or so it seems to have transpired), which would imply the Aspire flyers (which the entire of Aspire admires) are doing what we desire!
